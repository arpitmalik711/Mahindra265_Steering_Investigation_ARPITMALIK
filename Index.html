01 — Project Overview

Why Ackermann Geometry Matters

In a turn, each front wheel traces a different arc. To eliminate tyre scrub, the inner wheel must steer at a larger angle than the outer — the Ackermann condition. Pure Ackermann requires steering arms to converge at the rear axle centreline, a constraint rarely met in production vehicles due to packaging and structural limitations.

Agricultural tractors operate at low speeds in high-slip soil, where Ackermann deviation matters less than in highway vehicles. This study quantifies how closely the Mahindra 265 meets the ideal condition.

Methodology: analytical Ackermann model validated against smartphone inclinometer field data — a low-cost but rigorous approach applicable to any production vehicle assessment.

Engineering Insight

Why Camber-Out? — The Packaging Problem

A tight clearance at the kingpin top joint forced a design decision. Two solutions existed — only one preserved steering effort.

The Constraint

Red mark shows the critically tight gap between the kingpin top joint and the tyre sidewall. At 0° camber this gap closes — packaging fails.

Option A — Rejected
Shift the Entire Wheel Outward

Increasing wheel offset moves the whole wheel assembly outward — the tyre clears the kingpin joint. Problem solved… or is it?

The Problem

The contact patch centre moves outward with the wheel. The KPI axis intersection on the ground stays fixed. The distance between them — the scrub radius — increases. On a manual steering tractor with no power assist, this directly translates to heavier steering effort. Unacceptable.

Option B — Chosen ✓
Camber the Wheel Outward +3°

Tilting the wheel top outward by 3° pushes the tyre crown away from the kingpin joint — the clearance problem is solved without moving the wheel laterally.

Why It Works

The contact patch centre barely shifts laterally. The KPI axis intersection stays the same. Therefore scrub radius is largely unchanged — steering effort is preserved. The clearance is gained "for free" at the top of the tyre. Elegant.

Key Takeaway

Camber angle rotates the tyre about the contact patch — the crown moves out, the patch stays. Wheel offset shift moves everything out together. That distinction is why one solution works and the other doesn't.

06 — Theoretical Steering Model

Ackermann Formulation

Ideal inner wheel angle δi as a function of outer wheel angle δo, wheelbase L, and track t:

cot(δi) = cot(δo) − t / L
WHERE: L = 1905 mm  |  t = 1270 mm

The cotangent relationship is non-linear — the ideal curve progressively diverges from parallel steering as lock increases.

09 — Steering Arm Geometry Investigation

Arm Extension & Ackermann Construction

Extending the steering arm centrelines determines whether they converge at the rear axle — the criterion for pure Ackermann.

Fig. 9 — Replace with steering arm extension / Ackermann construction diagram

SolidWorks extension of the steering arm centrelines shows their intersection lies forward of the rear axle — confirming under-Ackermann geometry.

The offset distance from the intersection to the rear axle directly sets the degree of under-Ackermann. Longer arms or a larger toe-in angle would shift the intersection rearward and increase Ackermann tendency.

Forward intersection → under-Ackermann. At rear axle → pure Ackermann. Behind rear axle → over-Ackermann. Mahindra 265: mild-to-moderate under-Ackermann.

11 — Scrub Radius Estimation

Steering Axis Projection at Ground Plane

Horizontal offset between the steering axis ground intercept and tyre contact patch centre. Drives steering effort and self-centring torque.

Fig. 12 — Replace with steering axis projection / scrub radius diagram

KPI 7.75° + camber +3.0° → steering axis intercepts ground inboard of contact patch. Estimated positive scrub radius: ~32 mm.

Positive scrub radius creates a moment about the steering axis under tractive/braking loads — increases steering effort, felt most in manual systems at slow field speeds.

~32 mm positive scrub radius is acceptable for a compact beam-axle manual-steering tractor at agricultural working speeds.

12 — Engineering Discussion

Design Interpretation

Deviations observed are consistent with known production vehicle steering trade-offs.

Packaging Constraints

Pure Ackermann requires arms to converge at the rear axle — geometrically impractical in a compact beam-axle layout without sacrificing ground clearance or structural stiffness.

Manufacturing Tolerance

Casting tolerances in arm and axle geometry introduce systematic angular offsets. Acceptable for an off-highway vehicle with no high-speed steering demands.

Agricultural Operating Regime

At field working speeds on soft soil, slip angle effects dominate over kinematic Ackermann deviation — reducing its practical significance considerably.

Steering Effort — Manual System

Manual steering system. Positive scrub radius increases effort at lock but improves self-centring. KPI and camber tuned as a deliberate compromise between the two.

Positive Camber Implications

Positive camber pre-loads the outer bearing in straight running and partially unloads it in turns — suitable for field conditions where tyre wear rate is low.

Progressive Ackermann Tendency

Near-parallel at low lock → approaches ideal at mid-range → under-Ackermann at full lock. Geometry appears optimised for headland turns, not maximum lock manoeuvres.

13 — Measurement Uncertainty

Sources of Error and Limitations

📐
Smartphone Sensor Accuracy

Consumer MEMS accelerometers: ±0.3–0.5° resolution. Averaging reduces random error but leaves an irreducible ±0.5° uncertainty band on all reported angles.

📷
Photogrammetry Assumptions

CAD reconstruction assumes perpendicular camera alignment and zero lens distortion. Perspective error contributes ~±0.5° to KPI measurement.

🔄
Tyre Deformation Effects

Sidewall deformation under load alters apparent wheel plane orientation. High-aspect-ratio agricultural tyres can contribute up to ±1° apparent camber variation.

⚙️
Steering Linkage Compliance

Wear in drag link, tie rod ends, and kingpin bearings introduces non-systematic lash — cannot be corrected by averaging alone.

16 — Engineering Conclusion

Summary of Findings

Steering geometry of the Mahindra 265 (2012) was characterised through dimensional survey, SolidWorks photogrammetry, and smartphone inclinometer field tests.

Theoretical model (L=1905 mm, t=1270 mm) vs measured data reveals a progressive Ackermann tendency — near-parallel at low lock, approaching ideal at mid-range, and ~4–6° under-Ackermann at full lock.

KPI 7.75°, camber +3.0°, estimated scrub radius ~32 mm. Deviations from pure Ackermann are consistent with beam-axle packaging constraints and are well-suited to low-speed, high-slip field operation.
